Generic workflow for reducing any piece of academic work — draft article, grant application, chapter, one-page sketch — to a four-part structural map. **Descriptive only: no assessment, no suggestions, no rewriting.** This is a different move from [[tools:peer-review]]'s evaluative assessment: the map extracts what the document _says about itself_, and stops there.

## Provenance

The four-part frame (Aims / Methodology / Analytical theory / Contribution) is Nic's own, stated 2026-08-22. It was derived from his own highlighting of the DSA transparency FWF grant proposal PAT4902226 (Spencer-Smith) — see [[proposal]] and the completed referee report [[draft-review]] — where the clauses he highlighted were exactly the dot points he wanted pulled out. That highlighting pass is the origin case and the worked example below.

**This is Nic's preferred output shape for reading someone else's academic work generally**: a descriptive structural map, not an assessment. `Not stated` under a heading is a valid and useful answer — an empty heading is information, not a gap to fill by inference. This is a distinct mode from the sober assessor register of [[mem-f9f741f9]] (Nic's peer-review voice) and from the lighter-weight _evaluative_ collegial read proposed in [[aops_c6ea7823]] — that task wants a faster version of judgment (must-fix vs worth-thinking-about); this template wants _no_ judgment at all, only extraction.

## Input

One document, any length or stage of completion.

## Output

Four headed lists, one screen total. Each bullet is a **single clause lifted from the source**, trimmed to the claim and nothing else. Keep the author's own words wherever they exist — paraphrase only to make a fragment grammatical, never to improve it.

Cap each section at 4 bullets. If more candidates exist, keep the ones the rest of the document depends on.

### 1. Aims

What the work sets out to do, in its own framing. The object of study, and the question asked of it.

### 2. Methodology

What will actually be done to produce evidence: design, materials, methods, cases, data sources. Include scope decisions (which actors, which period, which jurisdiction) — they are method, not detail.

### 3. Analytical theory

The lens: the concepts through which the evidence will be read, and the tradition being drawn on or argued with. Often stated as a re-framing — "treats X as Y rather than Z".

### 4. Contribution

What is new, stated as a delta against existing work: empirical (covers cases nobody has), methodological (does it a way nobody has), theoretical (changes how the thing is understood). Separate the three when the document distinguishes them.

## Rules

- **Extract, don't infer.** If the document doesn't state it, write `Not stated` under that heading. An empty heading is the most useful thing the map produces.
- **Distinguish claimed from demonstrated.** Contribution bullets record what the author _claims_ is new. Do not check the claim; that is a separate pass (that separate pass is where [[tools:peer-review]] belongs).
- **Ignore the section structure.** Aims, method and theory are scattered through prose; in a well-written summary paragraph, all four appear within a few sentences of each other. The abstract or the closing paragraph of the introduction usually carries the whole map.
- **One pass, no dialogue.** Produce the map, then stop.

## Worked example

Source: DSA transparency proposal PAT4902226, §1 closing paragraph (see [[proposal]]).

**Aims**

- Investigates how implementation of the DSA sheds light on transparency as a means of mediating platform power in content moderation.
- Explores how different actors — major platforms, national regulators, civil society — interpret, implement and contest transparency requirements.

**Methodology**

- Mixed methods: comparative qualitative analysis of implementation documents; surveys and interviews with regulators and platforms; longitudinal analysis of the DSA Transparency Database.
- Scope extended to underexamined actors: European-based platforms and European regulatory bodies.

**Analytical theory**

- Treats the DSA transparency regime as a space where actors negotiate and institutionalise competing ideas of platform governance.
- Treats transparency as a dynamic, negotiated process rather than a static regulatory outcome.

**Contribution**

- _Methodological_: novel longitudinal method leveraging NLP over the Transparency Database.
- _Empirical_: coverage of European platforms and regulators beyond the usual major US platforms.
- _Theoretical_: a typology of transparency models, and an account of transparency not as regulation but as a structure through which actors assert and consolidate power.
