# Analytical Probes

The analytical core of a review: the recurring questions a careful assessor asks of _any_
application, independent of scheme. PREP reads the application against these probes and
drafts criterion comments from what they surface.

**Keep all eleven, verbatim.** They are one academic's distilled review style, held exactly
as written because condensing or merging probes silently drops analytical moves — extend the
set with probes your field needs, never prune it.

**Adapting the set.** The inline examples (jurisdictions, dollar figures, sample sizes,
"9 workshops", "four jurisdictions × three domains") come from law and social-science
grants. Substitute your own discipline's analogues, and treat the set as a strong default
that is untested on, say, biomedical or physics applications.

## The eleven probes

> ## What Nic consistently probes (the analytical signature)
>
> 1. **Theory of change / the missing bridge** — how do the empirical activities actually produce the normative, policy, or design outcomes promised? "The middle bit seems to be missing" is the most common fatal flaw. Descriptive predictors → normative guidance is asserted, not designed.
> 2. **Regulatory target clarity** — what exactly is being regulated, who is the addressee, and does the comparative jurisdiction choice serve the question?
> 3. **Phenomenon vs research project** — "a phenomenon, not a research project". Studying something new is not in itself a contribution; what is the sharply posed conceptual problem?
> 4. **Conceptual contribution vs descriptive output** — surveys/interviews that produce "some understanding of X" without theoretical payoff score D.
> 5. **Harm/problem assumed rather than demonstrated** — claims of urgency or safety risk must be grounded in literature or evidence, not asserted.
> 6. **Construct validity & method-question fit** — e.g. population surveys can't "test ethical frameworks in practice"; attitudes to hypothetical technologies are unstable; sample sizes vs claims ("the norms and values of Australians" from 9 workshops).
> 7. **Scope vs resourcing** — four jurisdictions × three domains on one casual RA; ambition vs delivery risk.
> 8. **Citation integration** — literature clumped into example-bursts vs genuinely integrated scaffolding.
> 9. **Access and dependency risk** — industry/regulator access assumed without groundwork or fallback.
> 10. **Real translation pathways** — advisory boards, named regulator engagement, prior legislative impact count; "public-facing resources" alone don't.
> 11. **Institutional commitment quality** — cash vs in-kind, teaching loads that undercut research intensity, transition risk.

## Using the probes in PREP

- Run every probe against the application; not all will fire, but each must be _considered_.
- Each probe that fires becomes an evidence-anchored observation in the reading notes
  (line-cited) and, where material, a reservation in a criterion comment.
- Probes 1, 3, 4 and 6 most often surface the **single largest analytical vulnerability**
  (the missing theory-of-change bridge; the descriptive output dressed as a conceptual
  contribution; the method that can't measure the claimed construct). Name that one
  explicitly in the notes — it usually decides where the score lands.
- Probes 7 and 9 are the **feasibility/access** pair: scope against funded effort, and
  assumed access without groundwork or fallback. Keep them distinct — collapsing them is
  how "access risk" disappears under "it's a big project".
- Probe 5 guards against accepting the application's framing: a stated harm or urgency must
  be grounded in evidence, not asserted. This is the agent's anti-credulity check.
- Aim feasibility and resourcing critiques (7, 11) at _institutions and structures_, never
  at a vulnerable early-career researcher (see [[review-guidance]]).
