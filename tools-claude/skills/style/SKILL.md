# Personal Writing Style Guide

Produce a working document that lets the writer — or an agent writing for them — reproduce
their voice. The guide is finished only when the writer confirms a test draft sounds like
them, not when the document looks complete.

## 1. Collect samples

Ask for 5–10 pieces the writer considers authentically theirs: any format, spanning
professional and personal and formal and casual contexts, recent enough to represent their
current voice. If every sample comes from one context, ask for more before analysing — a
guide built on one register mis-fires in every other one.

Read the whole set through once before analysing any of it.

## 2. Analyse the set

Characterise, across all samples:

- **Tone** — directness, formality (fixed or context-dependent), how they assert expertise,
  and the relationship they take with the reader.
- **Sentence and paragraph shape** — length, density, and how pieces open and close.
- **Argument structure** — how logic develops, what counts as evidence for them, how they
  explain hard things, how they move between ideas.
- **Word choice** — vocabulary register, enthusiasm markers, hedging against assertion,
  humour.
- **Recurring language** — the specific words, transitions, agreement and disagreement
  moves, and example-introductions that appear across multiple samples. Record these
  verbatim; a paraphrase of someone's habitual phrase is not their phrase.
- **Formatting habits** — lists against prose, how they emphasise, paragraph length.
- **Context adaptation** — what changes between audiences, platforms, and registers.
- **Anti-patterns** — what is conspicuously absent: common phrases they never use, openings
  and closings they avoid, industry-standard formulas they skip. Absence is as diagnostic as
  presence, and it is what stops a generic draft passing as theirs.

Record only what is distinctive to this voice. Universal writing advice in a personal style
guide crowds out the signal that makes it useful.

## 3. Draft the guide

```markdown
# {Name}'s Writing Style

## Core voice characteristics

### 1. {Quality}

**Shows up as**: {how it appears in their writing}
**Example**: "{verbatim quote from a sample}"
**In practice**: {what to do to reproduce it}

(3–5 qualities.)

## Language

**Uses**: "{their actual phrases}", by context
**Transitions**: "{their connectors}"
**Never uses**: "{phrases absent from every sample}"
**Instead of "{common alternative}" they write**: "{their version}"

## Structure

Opening / development / evidence / closing patterns. Emphasis technique. Lists against
prose. Paragraph length.

## Context adaptations

Professional against personal; per platform; per audience expertise.

## Voice examples

Verbatim quotes from two or more contexts, plus generic-against-their-voice pairs.

## Self-editing checklist
```

Every example must be a real string from a sample. An invented illustration teaches the
wrong voice.

## 4. Test, then refine

Write a 2–3 paragraph piece using the draft guide, on a scenario relevant to the writer's
work. Ask them what sounds authentically like them, what feels off, what is missing, what
you over-weighted, and which words here they would never use — asking for specific quotes
rather than impressions.

Update the guide from their answers: correct inaccurate characteristics, add missing
patterns, drop what you over-weighted, and add every phrase they rejected to the
anti-patterns list. Then write a fresh test piece and ask again. Repeat until they confirm
it reads as theirs; do not skip this loop, because sample analysis alone reliably produces
a guide that is plausible and wrong.

## 5. Deliver

Hand over the refined guide with a short note on using it: share the whole document with
any writing tool, check drafts against the "never uses" list before publishing, and share
the relevant sections with editors or collaborators.
